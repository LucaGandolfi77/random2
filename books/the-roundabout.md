# The Roundabout

*A novella in seven laps*

by Luca Gandolfi

> For anyone who has ever taken the long way home.

---

## I. The First Lap

The third exit was the one that went home. Elio Moretti had been avoiding it for three weeks.

He takes the fourth exit instead, the one that leads nowhere in particular, and the little green Panda leans into the curve like an old horse that knows the way. The roundabout is not much: a circle of asphalt, a patch of grass, a stone memorial with a bayonet on top and a pigeon on the bayonet. In the middle of the afternoon there is usually a car from the comune parked on the gravel and a man in orange trousers smoking. Today there is only the pigeon.

Elio goes around once. Twice. The indicator clicks, though he does not remember deciding to use it. At the third exit he does not turn. He looks at it as he passes: the road narrowing between two rows of poplars, the white house with the green shutters, the fig tree he can see from here if he leans forward. His house. He has lived in it for fifty-one years. He knows what is in it. That is the problem.

The package came on a Tuesday, three weeks ago. The postman, a young man with a tattoo of a swallow on his forearm, rang the bell and handed him a brown paper parcel tied with string. The handwriting was a woman's, round and careful, with a Bologna postmark. Elio signed the slip. "From a relative?" the postman asked. "A cousin," Elio said. He had no cousin in Bologna. The postman waited a moment, as if he could smell the lie, then shrugged and went back to his van. Elio carried the parcel into the kitchen. He put it on the oilcloth. He made coffee. He sat down. He stood up. He carried the parcel out to the Panda and put it in the trunk, under the blanket and the tin of motor oil. Then he drove to the roundabout and went around it four times before he understood what he was doing. He has not opened it since.

The handwriting on the parcel was not familiar. It was not Ada's. Ada's handwriting had been slanted and quick, the letters leaning forward as if they were in a hurry. This handwriting was round and careful, the letters standing upright, the ink blue. He had looked at it for a long time on the doorstep. He had thought: someone has taken trouble over this. Then he had put it in the trunk.

At home, the kitchen table is too big. Rina's chair is still on the other side. The parcel would sit on the oilcloth and wait for him to be a braver man.

So he drives.

He drives the same route every morning. Past the cemetery, past the station, past the bakery, past the garage that used to be his father's. He could do it with his eyes closed. He has done it with his eyes closed, once, on a dare, when he was seventeen. His father had boxed his ears. He had laughed. The town has changed since then: the cinema is a supermarket, the school has a new roof, the young people leave. The roundabout is still the same. The pigeon is still there. The bayonet is still there. The stone memorial has a new plaque, but no one reads it.

The Panda smells of dust and peppermint. A ceramic swallow hangs from the mirror on a red thread; Rina bought it at the fair in Fidenza the year they retired. It turns slowly as he drives, showing first its white belly, then its blue back. Elio finds this unbearable and comforting in equal measure. The car is the color of a dead leaf. There is a crack in the dashboard that looks like the outline of a river. He has never fixed it.

Rina used to say the Panda was held together by habit. She was right. The passenger door only opens from the outside, the radio only gets Radio Maria, and the heater has two settings: off and August. He has been meaning to buy a new car for ten years. He cannot imagine sitting in a different seat.

On the fifth lap he begins to count. On the ninth he loses count. He is not lost. A man who goes around a roundabout is not lost; he is postponing a decision. There is a difference, though he could not explain it to the carabinieri if they stopped him.

At the bus stop on the first exit, a boy and a girl are kissing. The boy has his hand in the girl's hair. Elio looks away, then looks back. He is eighty-three. He is not jealous. He is not even sad, exactly. He is tired of the way the past keeps arriving uninvited, like a relative who has lost his key.

At the fourth exit, Teresa Bissi is standing at the gate of her vegetable garden with a hoe in her hand. Teresa is eighty-six and has lived on the road since before the war. She has watched Elio go around the roundabout every morning for three weeks. She raises the hoe. He slows down. "Elio," she says. "Are you going to the hardware store?" "Yes," he says. "The hardware store." "You went yesterday." "I forgot something." She looks at the Panda. She looks at the road behind him. "You know," she says, "the road doesn't change if you go around it enough times." He laughs, because she is right and because he does not want to explain. "I like the view," he says. She shakes her head and goes back to her beans. In the mirror, he sees her standing there, watching him go, and he understands that by tomorrow the whole street will know.

He does not think her name. He has not thought her name in years. But the roundabout has a way of loosening things. A fountain. A blue dress. A bottle of aranciata standing in the water like a candle. The smell of gasoline on a girl's hands.

Ada Caselli. There. He has thought it.

The parcel is in the trunk, under a blanket and a tin of motor oil. It has been there for three weeks. It has become a fact of the car, like the spare tire. Sometimes, at a red light, he imagines opening it. He imagines the paper tearing, the string falling away, the past spilling onto the passenger seat. Then the light turns green and he drives on.

He takes the fourth exit again. The road to nowhere is a good road. It runs along the fields, past a farmhouse with a collapsed roof, past a ditch full of reeds, past the place where the river used to flood in November. He drives for twenty minutes and then turns back, because there is nowhere to go.

The long way home takes him past the cemetery. He does not stop. The gate is open. Someone has left a jar of flowers on the wrong grave. He knows the woman who does it: she is old and confused and believes her husband is in every row. Elio has never corrected her. It seems unkind to be right about such a thing. Rina is not there. Rina is in the kitchen, in the blue cardigan on the hook, in the geraniums on the windowsill that he waters every morning whether they need it or not. Rina is in the way he still makes two cups of coffee and then pours one down the sink.

At the house, he parks the Panda in the driveway and sits for a moment with his hands on the wheel. The engine ticks as it cools. The swallow turns on its thread.

He gets out. He does not open the trunk.

The garden is doing what gardens do in May: the tomatoes are climbing their stakes, the fig tree has small hard fruit, the geraniums are red and shameless. He waters them. He stands in the grass with the hose in his hand and the water running over his shoes, and he thinks about the parcel.

Rina planted the first geraniums the spring after they moved in. She had bought six small pots at the market and carried them home in a cardboard box. "They'll die," Elio had said. "Everything dies," she had said. "That's not a reason not to plant it." The geraniums had not died. They had multiplied. Every spring he bought new ones and every autumn he cut them back. He did not know why he kept doing it. He knew exactly why.

Then he goes inside, makes coffee, and does not drink it. The clock on the kitchen wall runs fast. It has always run fast. Rina used to say it was because the house was in a hurry to be happy. He never understood what she meant, but he liked the way she said it.

He sits at the table until the light goes orange, then gray, then blue. The parcel is in the car. The car is in the driveway. The driveway is ten meters from the kitchen door. He could go out and get it. He could open it. He could read whatever is inside and then he would know, and knowing would be worse than not knowing, or better, or the same. He does not move.

At nine o'clock he goes to bed. He sleeps on the left side, though there is no reason anymore. In his dream, water is falling in a courtyard, and someone is laughing, and he cannot see her face. The courtyard is the one in Bologna, the one he never saw from the inside. Water falls from a gutter onto the stones. Ada is laughing somewhere above him, behind a window he cannot find. He calls her name, but the water is too loud. He wakes with his hand on the empty side of the bed.

In the morning he will drive to the roundabout again. He knows this the way he knows the route to the cemetery: without deciding. The third exit will be there, patient as a closed door.

## II. The Fountain

The summer Ada Caselli came to San Prospero, the fountain in Piazza Grande had been broken for eleven days.

It was July, and the town was full of heat. The awnings over the shops hung limp. The pigeons walked instead of flying. Old men sat in the shade of the portico and argued about football with their eyes closed. The comune had promised to fix the fountain before the Festa della Madonna, and the comune, as usual, had done nothing.

The fountain had been built in 1889 by a committee of citizens who wanted the town to have something to look at while they argued. It had three lions' heads, a central jet, and a basin wide enough for children to sail paper boats in. For seventy years it had worked without complaint. Then the pump failed, and the comune discovered that the replacement part came from Milan and cost more than the annual budget for street cleaning. The mayor gave a speech. The priest blessed the dry basin. The pigeons used it as a meeting room. The old men in the portico said the town had been going downhill since the war, and then they went back to their cards.

Elio Moretti was nineteen years old and worked in his father's garage on the Via Emilia. He had grease under his nails and a permanent smell of gasoline in his clothes. He was tall and thin and had a nose his mother called a gift from the Morettis, which was her way of saying it was too big. He could take an engine apart and put it back together without looking at the manual. He could not talk to girls without looking at his shoes.

His father, Pietro Moretti, had opened the garage in 1948 with a borrowed compressor and a sign he had painted himself. He was a small, square man with a mustache like a shoe brush and a belief that a man who could not fix his own bicycle did not deserve a car. He had taught Elio to listen to an engine the way a doctor listens to a chest. "The motor tells you everything," he said. "You just have to stop talking." Elio's mother, Anna, kept the accounts in a ledger with a blue cover and made him wash his hands before lunch, a rule he obeyed until he was seventeen and then ignored for the rest of his life. The garage smelled of oil, rubber, and the cigarettes his father pretended not to smoke. On the wall hung a calendar with a girl on a tire and a photograph of the national cycling team. The radio was always on, quietly, tuned to the news or the opera. Elio had grown up in that smell and that sound. He could not imagine a life without them.

On the morning of the twelfth day, his father handed him a wrench and a slip of paper. "The fountain," he said. "The comune called. Go and make it work."

The fountain stood in the middle of the piazza, a stone basin with three lions' heads and a central jet that had not sprayed since June. When Elio arrived, a girl was sitting on the rim with her legs crossed and a book open on her knees. She wore a blue cotton dress. Her hair was dark and pinned up badly, as if she had done it in a hurry. A bottle of aranciata stood in the water beside her, cooling.

She looked up. She had a face that was not beautiful in the way the magazines meant, but it was the kind of face you kept looking at. Her eyes were gray. Her mouth was already amused.

"Are you the plumber?" she asked.

"I'm the mechanic," Elio said.

"That's a shame. I was hoping for a plumber."

He knelt by the pump housing and opened the panel. The girl watched him. He could feel her watching him. He dropped the wrench, picked it up, and dropped it again.

"You're nervous," she said.

"I'm not."

"You are. You dropped the wrench."

"The wrench is old."

She laughed. It started low and came out all at once, like a drawer opening. "My name is Ada," she said. "Ada Caselli. I'm staying with my aunt Iole for the summer. She lives above the bakery. She told me the fountain was broken and that the comune would never fix it, and I said I would sit here until they did."

"You've been sitting here for eleven days?"

"Don't be silly. I've been sitting here for an hour. But I would have stayed eleven days."

Elio took off the pump cover. The mechanism was clogged with leaves and a small, desiccated frog. He cleaned it out. Ada put her book down and came to stand beside him. She smelled of soap and something else, something green, like the inside of a fig.

"What are you reading?" he asked.

"*Il Gattopardo*. Do you know it?"

"No."

"It's about a prince who thinks he can keep the past by letting it go. I don't understand him. He's a coward."

"Maybe he's right," Elio said. "Maybe some things can't be kept."

Ada looked at him for a moment. "Maybe," she said. "But I don't think a coward would know the difference."

He turned the valve. The pump coughed, shuddered, and then the water came up in a thin, uncertain arc. It fell back into the basin with a sound like applause. Ada clapped her hands once, twice, three times. The pigeons lifted off the memorial. A man in a shop doorway put down his newspaper and looked.

"You fixed it," she said.

"It was the frog."

She picked up her aranciata, wiped the bottle on her dress, and held it out to him. "Have some. You've earned it."

Elio did not know where to put his eyes. He took the bottle. Their fingers touched. The bottle was cold. The drink was sweet and sharp, and it tasted like the beginning of something.

"Thank you," he said.

"Elio," she said, trying his name. "Like the sun."

"Like the gas," he said.

She laughed again. "I like you, Elio Moretti."

He looked at his shoes. They were covered in grease. "I like you too," he said, so quietly that he was not sure he had said it.

The next day she came to the garage. His father was out on a call. Ada stood in the doorway in a yellow dress and watched Elio change the oil in a Fiat 600. She asked questions: what did this part do, why did that one make that noise, what happened if you forgot to put the oil back. He answered. He showed her how to check the dipstick. She put her hand on his to steady the rag. Her fingers were cool. He forgot the rest of the sentence.

"Your hands are clean now," she said.

"They won't be for long."

"Good," she said. "I like them better dirty."

The next evening Ada took him to meet her aunt. The bakery was on Via Garibaldi, a narrow street off the piazza, with a wooden sign that said FORNO IOLE in letters faded to the color of flour. The shop was closed, but the door was open, and the smell came out into the street like a welcome. Ada led him up a dark staircase to a kitchen where a small woman with gray braids stood at a table, punching down dough.

"Aunt Iole," Ada said, "this is Elio. The mechanic."

Iole looked at him the way a customs officer looks at a suitcase. She was seventy, or maybe a hundred; it was hard to tell. She had a baker's arms, strong and floury, and eyes that missed nothing.

"The mechanic," she said. "Can you fix a stove?"

"I can try."

"Good. The left burner has been sulking since Easter."

He fixed the burner. It took twenty minutes and a screwdriver Ada found in a drawer full of string. When the flame came on, blue and steady, Iole nodded once.

"All right," she said. "Sit. You'll eat."

She cut him a slice of warm bread and spread it with butter and honey. It was the best thing he had ever tasted. Ada sat across from him with her chin in her hand, watching him eat. Iole asked about his father, his mother, his wages, his intentions, and whether he went to Mass. He answered honestly, which seemed to surprise her. When he left, she gave him a loaf of bread wrapped in paper.

"For your mother," she said. "Tell her I asked."

At the door, Ada kissed him on the cheek. "She likes you," she said.

"She said I eat too fast," Elio said.

"That's how she says she likes you."

A week before the festa, Elio stayed late at the garage. He found a brass washer in a box of odds and ends, small and flat, the size of a coin. He polished it on the buffing wheel until it shone, then drilled a hole through the center and threaded it onto a piece of red string. It was not a ring. He knew it was not a ring. But it was something to put around her neck, and it had come from his hands, and that seemed important.

He gave it to her in the doorway of the bakery, with his heart going fast. Ada turned it over in her palm.

"It's a washer," she said.

"It's from an engine," he said. "A good engine."

She put it around her neck and tucked it under her dress. "I'll wear it until it turns my skin green."

"That might be soon."

"I don't mind."

That Sunday he asked his father for the Vespa. His father looked at him over the top of his glasses. "You'll pay for the petrol," he said. "And if you break it, you'll pay for that too."

The Vespa was sky blue with a dent in the front fender and a seat that had been repaired with black tape. Elio drove Ada out of town, along the road that followed the Stirone. The poplars made a corridor of light and shadow. She sat behind him with her arms around his waist. He could feel her chin on his shoulder. He drove slowly. He wanted the ride to last.

They stopped by the river. Ada took off her shoes and walked into the water up to her ankles. She said it was cold. She said he should come in. He said someone had to watch the Vespa. She said the Vespa could watch itself. He took off his shoes and went in. The water was cold. The stones were smooth. A heron lifted from the far bank and went away over the fields.

"What do you want?" she asked.

"From what?"

"From life."

No one had ever asked him that. He thought about it. The river made its small continuous sound. "A garage of my own," he said. "With my name on it. A house with a garden. A dog, maybe."

"That's all?"

"That's a lot."

She nodded. "I want a classroom. A window with geraniums. Children who ask impossible questions." She looked at him. "And someone to come home to."

Elio looked at the water. He thought: I could give her a house with geraniums. He did not say it. He was nineteen, and the words were too big for his mouth.

On another afternoon at the river, the weather turned. One minute the sky was white with heat and the next it was green, and the rain came across the fields like a curtain pulled by a hand. They ran for the bridge with their shoes in their hands, laughing, and stood under the arch while the water darkened and the poplars bent. Ada's dress was soaked. She wrung out the hem and laughed at the sound it made. The rain drummed on the bridge above them. Elio took off his shirt and put it around her shoulders.

"You'll be cold," she said.

"I'm not cold."

She stepped closer. He could feel the heat of her through the wet cotton. She put her hand flat on his chest, as if she were listening to an engine.

"Your heart is going fast," she said.

"It does that."

"Since when?"

"Since the fountain."

She kissed him then, not the way she had kissed him on the cheek, and the rain kept falling, and the river kept rising, and for a moment the whole world was the small dry space under the bridge.

On the way back, they passed the roundabout. He slowed the Vespa and pointed at the exits. "First one goes to the cemetery," he said. "Second to the station. Third goes home. Fourth goes nowhere."

Ada leaned her cheek against his back. "Which one are we taking?"

"The long way," he said.

She laughed and held him tighter.

When he dropped her off at her aunt's door, she kissed him on the cheek. Then she stepped back and looked at him. "There's a dance on Saturday," she said. "The Festa della Madonna. Will you ask me?"

"Yes," he said.

"Good." She went inside. The door closed. Elio stood in the street for a long time, smelling gasoline and jasmine, listening to the fountain in the piazza.

On Thursday, Ada came to dinner at the Moretti house. She wore a green dress and brought a cake from Iole's bakery and a bottle of wine that cost more than the Morettis' Sunday roast. Anna had cleaned the house twice. Pietro had put on a clean shirt and combed his mustache. They ate at the kitchen table, which was too small for four, and Ada sat between Elio and his mother and ate everything she was given.

Anna asked about her family. Ada said her father was a notary in Bologna. Anna's fork paused. Pietro asked if the notary had a car.

"A Lancia," Ada said.

Pietro nodded slowly, as if this were a fact that would need to be considered later.

"And your mother?" Anna asked.

"She died when I was twelve," Ada said.

The table went quiet. Ada looked at her plate, then at Anna. "I remember her hands," she said. "She played the piano. I don't play. I wish I did."

Anna reached over and touched her wrist, just for a moment. "You'll learn," she said.

"Or you'll teach," Elio said, and everyone laughed, and the room loosened.

After dinner, Pietro took Ada out to the garage and showed her the engine he was rebuilding. Elio watched from the door as his father explained the valves, and Ada listened with her head tilted, asking questions. When she left, Anna stood at the window and watched her walk to the corner.

"She's not like us," she said.

"No," Elio said. "She's better."

Anna turned. "Don't be stupid," she said, but she was smiling. "She's a girl who has never had to worry about money. That's not better. It's just different. Be kind to her. And don't let your father talk to her about carburetors for more than an hour."

## III. The Dance

The piazza was strung with lights. Paper bulbs hung between the portico columns, yellow and red and blue, and a band from Parma had set up on a platform in front of the church. The air smelled of fried dough, wine, and the dust that rose from the feet of children running in circles. The Festa della Madonna had begun.

The festa came every year on the second weekend of July, and for three days the town pretended it was larger than it was. There were stalls selling sugared almonds, roasted nuts, and cheap jewelry that turned green by August. There was a raffle for a bicycle, a pig, and a hamper of wine. The church was open late, and the priest stood in the doorway in his black cassock, fanning himself with a bulletin. The band played waltzes and tangos and the new songs from the radio. The mayor made a speech that no one listened to. The children ran until they fell down. The old women sat on chairs in the shade and criticized the dresses. For three days the town was a place where something might happen.

At seven o'clock his father came into the kitchen and took his good jacket off the hook. He held it up.

"Put this on," he said.

Elio put it on. The shoulders hung past his own. His father looked at him for a long time.

"A jacket is not a man," he said. "But it helps."

He straightened the collar. "Don't talk about engines. Ask her questions. And when you dance, look at her face, not at your feet."

"I don't know how to dance."

"Nobody knows how to dance. You just move and don't apologize."

Anna pinned the cuffs and stepped back. "He's right," she said. "About the jacket, at least."

At nine o'clock Elio was standing by the fountain with a paper cone of sugared almonds, watching the crowd. He had polished his shoes until they reflected the lights. The jacket still felt like a borrowed life.

Ada came at nine. She wore a blue dress, the same blue as the dress she had worn the first day, but this one had a full skirt and a white collar. Her hair was pinned up, and a small white flower was tucked behind her ear. She saw him and smiled. The crowd seemed to move aside for her. Elio forgot how to breathe.

"You came," she said.

"You asked me to."

"That's not the same thing."

He held out the cone of almonds. "I brought you these."

She took one and put it in her mouth. "They're my favorite," she said. "How did you know?"

"I didn't. I guessed."

"Good guess."

The band began to play. It was "Quando, quando, quando," the song everyone was dancing to that summer. Ada held out her hand. "Come on."

"I'm not a good dancer."

"Neither am I. We'll be bad together."

She led him onto the piazza. He put his hand on her waist, carefully, as if she were made of something that could break. She moved closer and put her hand on his shoulder. The music was fast and sweet. He stepped on her foot. She laughed. He stepped on her foot again. She said, "Follow me." He followed. After a while he stopped counting the steps and began to feel the rhythm, and then he was dancing, badly but happily, under the paper lights.

After the first song he began to enjoy himself. The band played a waltz, then a tango, then something fast from Rome that the younger couples danced with their elbows out. Ada taught him the steps by moving his hands where she wanted them. She was a good teacher. She was also a merciless one. "You're thinking," she said. "Stop thinking." He stopped. The piazza tilted and spun and came back. The paper lights swung overhead. A boy in short trousers ran between the dancers selling paper cones of nuts. The mayor danced with the baker's wife. Two old men played cards on a barrel and ignored the music. Elio forgot the garage, the comune, the broken fountain, his own name. He remembered only the weight of her hand and the small brass washer turning at her throat.

At the edge of the piazza, near the bakery stall, Aunt Iole stood with her arms folded. She had come down to watch, though she pretended to be checking the shutters. When Elio looked at her, she raised one eyebrow. Then she nodded, once, the way she had nodded when the stove burner lit. Ada saw her too.

"She approves," Ada said.

"How can you tell?"

"She hasn't come over to send me home."

The song ended. Another began. They kept dancing. Ada's cheek was warm against his. He could feel the small bones of her wrist under his hand. She smelled of soap and jasmine and the sugared almonds. He thought: I will remember this when I am old. He was nineteen. It seemed like a very distant thing.

Between songs, they walked to the edge of the piazza and drank wine from a paper cup. Ada asked him about the garage. He told her about the Fiat 600 with the bad clutch and the old man who came in every week to have his tires checked even though they were fine. She told him about Bologna: the porticoes, the red roofs, her father's house on Via Santo Stefano, the window of her room that looked onto a courtyard with a lemon tree in a pot. She said her father was a notary and that he did not laugh much. She told him about her mother again, and he listened as if he had not heard it before. She said she did not want to be a notary.

She told him about the dinners where no one ate, the conversations about politics that were really about money, the way her father called her "my dear" when he was angry. She said she wanted a life where people said what they meant. Elio said his family said what they meant too much. She laughed and put her hand on his knee, and then took it away, as if she had remembered something.

"I want to teach," she said. "The little ones. Six years old. They still believe you when you tell them the world is good."

"Is it?"

"It can be." She looked at him. "It is tonight."

They danced until the band played the last song. Then the lights went out, one string at a time, and the piazza became dark and quiet. Ada took his hand and led him behind the church, where the fountain was still running and the air was cool. They sat on the stone steps. She leaned her head on his shoulder. The water made its small continuous sound.

"Will you write to me?" she asked. "When I go back?"

"Every day."

"Every day is a lot of letters."

"I'll write every Sunday," he said. "And you'll write back."

"I'll write back." She lifted her head. "Elio."

"Yes."

"I'm going to miss you."

He kissed her. It was not a practiced kiss. It was the kind of kiss that begins as a question and ends as an answer. She tasted of lemon and wine. He put his hand on her waist, and she said, "Piano." He stopped. She put her forehead against his.

"Not because I don't want to," she said. "Because I want to remember this."

He nodded. He did not trust his voice.

She reached into her hair and took out the white flower. It was a cornflower, slightly crushed. She pinned it to the lapel of his father's jacket. Then she took something from around her neck: a small brass washer on a piece of red string. He had given it to her the week before, polished on the buffing wheel in the garage. She had tied it around her neck and worn it every day since.

"You keep this," she said, putting it into his hand. "So you don't forget me."

"I won't forget you."

"I know. But keep it anyway."

He closed his hand around the washer. It was warm from her skin. "It's not a ring," he said.

"It's a promise that can be worn," she said. "That's better."

They sat on the steps until the church clock struck one. Then he walked her home. Her aunt's window was dark. At the door, she kissed him again, quickly, and went inside. Elio stood in the street with the cornflower on his jacket and the washer in his pocket.

The train left at six ten the next morning. He was there by five thirty. The station was cold and almost empty. A porter was asleep on a bench. The clock above the ticket window had a cracked face. Ada came at ten past five, with her aunt Iole in a gray coat carrying a paper bag of bread and cheese for the journey. Iole kissed Ada on both cheeks and said something in her ear. Ada nodded. Then Iole shook Elio's hand, which surprised him; her grip was strong.

"You'll write," she said. It was not a question.

"I'll write," he said.

Ada wore a traveling coat and carried a small suitcase. She looked tired and excited and very young. The platform smelled of coal and rain. A porter wheeled a cart of suitcases past them. The station clock said six oh eight and then six ten. Ada's eyes were bright. She was not crying. He was not crying either, though his throat felt full of gravel.

"I'll write," she said.

"I'll write first."

"You'd better."

He took the cornflower from his lapel and tucked it into her coat pocket. "So you don't forget," he said.

"I won't," she said.

The train came. She climbed the steps and turned in the doorway. She waved. He waved back. The doors closed. The train pulled away, and he stood on the platform until it was a small dark line, and then until it was nothing. Then he went back to the garage and worked on an engine that did not need fixing.

That night he sat at the kitchen table with the bottle of blue ink and a sheet of paper. He wrote "Dear Ada," and then sat looking at it for ten minutes. His mother came in, put a hand on his shoulder, and went out again. He wrote about the platform, the train, the empty garage, the way the town sounded after she left. He crossed out two sentences and copied the letter onto a fresh sheet. Then he wrote, "I already miss you. I am not going to pretend otherwise." He addressed the envelope, put on his jacket, and walked to the station to post it before he could change his mind. The night train came through at eleven, though it did not stop. He stood on the platform and watched its lights go away toward Bologna. Then he went home and slept, and in the morning he went back to the garage.

## IV. The Letters

Her first letter arrived on a Thursday, eleven days later. It was written on thin blue paper, the kind that folds into its own envelope. Her handwriting was slanted and quick, as if she were afraid of losing the thought before she got it down. She wrote about the train, the heat in Bologna, her father's silence at dinner, the lemon tree in the courtyard. She wrote that she had told her father about the mechanic from San Prospero. She wrote that he had said, "A mechanic," in a way that made her want to throw a plate at him. She wrote that she missed the fountain.

> *Dear Elio,*
>
> *The train was late and hot and a man in my compartment ate a whole salami. I thought of you the entire way. Bologna is the same: the porticoes are still cool, the lemon tree is still alive, my father is still disappointed in everything. At dinner I told him about the mechanic who fixed the fountain. He put down his fork and said, "A mechanic," as if you were a disease. I wanted to throw the plate at him. I didn't. I am saving my strength.*
>
> *I miss the fountain. I miss the way you looked at me when you thought I wasn't looking. Write to me. Tell me everything, even the boring things. Especially the boring things.*
>
> *Ada*

Elio read the letter four times. Then he put it in the drawer of his bedside table, under his socks, and went to work.

He wrote back that Sunday. He sat at the kitchen table with a bottle of blue ink and a sheet of paper from his father's stationery. He wrote about the garage, the Fiat 600, the cat that slept on the compressor, the price of tires. He wrote that the fountain was still working. He wrote that he had gone to the river on Sunday and the heron was back. He wrote that he missed her. He crossed out the last sentence, then wrote it again.

> *Dear Ada,*
>
> *The fountain is still working. I check it every morning on my way to the garage, which is a lie, because the garage is the other way, but I check it anyway. The cat has had kittens under the compressor. My father says we cannot keep them. We are keeping them.*
>
> *I don't know how to write letters. I keep starting sentences and crossing them out. What I want to say is that the town is smaller since you left. The piazza is the same size, but it feels smaller. I don't know how that works.*
>
> *I miss you. That is all I know how to say.*
>
> *Elio*

The letters went back and forth through the autumn. Ada wrote about her teacher training, the children she observed at the school, the professor who smelled of eucalyptus. She wrote about her father, who wanted her to marry the son of a colleague. She wrote that she had told him she would choose her own husband. Elio wrote about his father's cough, which was getting worse. He wrote about the garage and the rain and the way the town looked in November, gray and closed. He wrote that he was saving money. He did not say what for. She did not ask.

One of her letters was four pages long and smelled faintly of lemon. She wrote:

> *The children are impossible and wonderful. Today a boy asked me why the sky is blue and I gave him the scientific answer and he said, "But why blue and not green?" I have been thinking about it all evening. I think he is a genius or a lunatic. Perhaps both.*
>
> *My father has invited the son of a colleague to dinner on Sunday. The son has a mustache and an opinion about everything. I have told my father that I will choose my own husband. He said, "You are young." I said, "I am not." We are not speaking. It is peaceful.*

In December she wrote: "In the spring, come to Bologna. I want you to meet my father. I want him to see that I am not a child. I want him to see you."

Elio read the letter in the garage, under the light of a single bulb. He looked at the toolbox on the wall, the calendar with the girl on the tire, the Fiat 600 waiting for a new clutch. Then he went to the office and counted the money in the tin box. It was not enough for a ring, but it was enough for a train ticket and a good jacket.

He wrote back: "In April. I'll come in April."

In January, the letters stopped.

He wrote again. No answer. He wrote the following Sunday. No answer. He wrote seven letters in seven weeks. He told himself she was busy. He told himself the post was slow. He told himself her father had found the letters. He told himself she had met someone else. He told himself she was ill. He told himself she had never loved him. Each explanation was worse than the last.

In March he took the train to Bologna. He did not tell his father. He wore the good jacket and carried a small bag with a clean shirt and a comb. The train was crowded with students and salesmen. He sat by the window and watched the fields give way to factories, then to streets, then to the city.

He had never been to Bologna. The station was enormous and full of noise. He walked out into the porticoes and felt the city close over his head like a roof. The shops were full of things he could not afford. The women wore shoes that clicked. He found Via Santo Stefano by asking a man at a newsstand, who pointed with his chin and said, "Keep going until the street gets narrow." He walked past a church with a rose window, a bakery that smelled of anise, a courtyard with a bicycle leaning against a wall. He stopped at a café and ordered a coffee, then realized he did not know how much to tip. He left too much. The waiter did not seem surprised.

The house was old and narrow, with a green door and a brass knocker in the shape of a lion's head. He stood across the street for an hour. He rehearsed what he would say. He would say, "I am Elio. I fixed the fountain." He would say, "I love your daughter." He would say, "I have a trade and a Vespa and I am not afraid of work." None of the sentences sounded like him. He thought about crossing the street and knocking. He thought about it for a long time.

At six o'clock a light came on in the window on the first floor. He saw Ada. She was thinner than he remembered. She was setting the table. Her father sat in a chair by the window, reading a newspaper. Ada said something. Her father did not look up. She stood there for a moment with a plate in her hand, and then she turned away.

Elio did not knock. He told himself it was because he did not want to make a scene. He told himself it was because she looked tired. He told himself it was because a man should not beg. He told himself many things. None of them was the truth. The truth was that he was nineteen years old and afraid.

He wrote one more letter from San Prospero. "Ada, if you want me to stop, tell me. But don't leave me without an answer. I would rather know." He posted it on a Monday. On Friday, a single sheet of paper arrived. It was not her usual blue paper. It was white, typed, and it said:

"Please don't write to me anymore. I wish you well. Ada."

There was no explanation. There was no signature beyond her name. He looked at the paper for a long time. Then he put it in a shoebox with the other letters, tied the box with string, and put it on the top shelf of his wardrobe. He did not cry until he was alone in the garage that night, and then he cried under a car, which he later decided was a stupid place to cry.

The years are not a straight line. They arrive in pieces.

He met Rina at his cousin's wedding in 1965. She was a seamstress from Parma, with quick hands and a laugh that filled a room. She wore a red dress and danced with everyone. When she danced with Elio, she said, "You're thinking about something else." He said, "I'm thinking about the steps." She said, "Liar." Then she laughed, and he laughed too, and something in him that had been closed opened a little.

They married in 1966, in the church of San Prospero, with the same priest who had blessed the dry fountain. Rina wore a dress she had made herself. Elio's father coughed through the ceremony and afterward drank too much wine and told everyone that his son had married above himself. Anna cried and pretended it was the onions.

Marco was born in 1968, in August, during a heat wave. He came out red and furious and did not sleep for two years. Giulia was born in 1971, in the rain, and slept through everything, which worried Rina until the doctor said some babies were simply kind. Elio learned to change diapers on the hood of a car, to make a bottle with one hand, to sleep standing up. He taught Marco to ride a bicycle on the road to the roundabout, running behind him with a hand on the saddle until Marco shouted, "Let go!" and he let go, and Marco went wobbling down the road and did not fall. Giulia was quieter. She sat in the garage and watched him work, handing him tools before he asked for them. "She has the hands," Pietro said. "She should have been the mechanic." Elio did not disagree.

His father died in the winter of 1975, coughing until the end, and Elio took over the garage and painted his own name on the sign: *Officina Moretti*. They bought the house on the third exit road. Rina planted geraniums. They had a dog named Otto, who was afraid of the vacuum cleaner. They went to the sea every August and ate peaches on the balcony. It was a good life. It was not the life he had imagined when he was nineteen, but it was a good life, and he knew it.

One afternoon, when the children were small, Rina found the shoebox. She was cleaning the top shelf of the wardrobe. She climbed onto a chair and took down the box. She opened it, read one letter, and put it back. Elio was in the kitchen, eating bread and cheese. He heard the chair creak. He heard the lid close. He waited for her to come down. She did not come down for a long time.

She did not say anything at dinner. She did not say anything for three days. Then, on Sunday, while Elio was washing the car, she came out and stood beside him with her arms crossed.

"You were loved before me," she said.

Elio stopped with the sponge in his hand. "Rina—"

"I'm not angry." She looked at the car, not at him. "I'm saying it's all right. It means you know how."

He did not know what to say. He wanted to tell her about Ada, and about Bologna, and about the letter that said please don't write to me anymore. But the words would not come. He said, "I love you."

"I know," she said. "That's why I can say it."

She took the sponge from his hand and washed the other side of the car. They worked in silence. Then she said, "Was she pretty?"

"Yes."

"Good," she said. "I would have been annoyed if she was ugly."

She never mentioned the shoebox again. He never opened it again.

They lived another half century together. Rina got sick in the spring, five years ago. The hospitals were full of strangers in masks, and Elio could not visit her for three weeks. He stood outside with a bag of oranges and a photograph of the garden, and a nurse promised to take them in. When he finally saw her, she had lost weight and her hair was thin, but she was still Rina. "You look terrible," she said. "You should see yourself," he said. She laughed, and then coughed, and then laughed again.

She came home in June. The children came. Marco drove from Turin, Giulia from Florence. They sat in the garden under the fig tree and ate ice cream and pretended everything was normal. Rina directed the watering of the geraniums from a chair. In August she stopped eating. In September she slept most of the day. One morning she woke early and asked Elio to open the window. The air smelled of rain. "The geraniums need cutting back," she said. "I'll do it," he said. "I know," she said. "That's why I'm telling you."

She died on a Tuesday, in the afternoon, with Elio holding her hand and the radio playing something soft in the kitchen. He did not cry at the funeral. He cried three weeks later, in the supermarket, when he saw a jar of the jam she liked and realized he would never buy it again.

After the funeral, he found the box on the top shelf, still tied with string, and he did not open it. He put it back. He was not ready. He was not sure he would ever be ready.

Then, one morning in April, a package arrived.

## V. The Other Life

The woman at the door had Ada's eyes.

That was the first thing Elio noticed. Gray eyes, slightly tilted, with the same trick of looking amused before anything had been said. She was around fifty, with dark hair going gray at the temples, and she wore a green raincoat even though the sun was out. She held a jar of something in one hand and a handbag in the other. She had parked a small gray Fiat at the curb, half on the sidewalk, the way people parked in Bologna. Her voice had the flat, educated vowels of the city, but under them was something else, a faint roundness on the o's, as if she had learned to speak in one place and learned to live in another.

"Signor Moretti?" she said.

"Yes."

"My name is Clara Bellini. Ada Caselli was my mother."

Elio put his hand on the doorframe. The garden behind him was full of May: the tomatoes, the fig tree, the geraniums. The woman waited. She did not look away. She had Ada's habit of standing very still, as if stillness were a form of attention.

"Come in," he said.

They sat at the kitchen table. Clara put the jar on the oilcloth. "Apricot jam," she said. "My mother made it every June. There's still a case of it in the cellar. I'm trying to eat it before it goes bad."

Elio made coffee. His hands were steady, which surprised him. He put the cups on the table. Clara wrapped her fingers around hers. She looked around the kitchen: the clock, the blue cardigan on the hook, the photograph of Rina on the sideboard.

"I sent you a package," she said. "Three weeks ago."

"I know."

"You haven't opened it."

It was not a question. Elio looked at the table. "No."

Clara nodded. She did not seem surprised. "My mother said you would take your time."

"How did she—" He stopped.

"She knew you." Clara smiled, and it was Ada's smile, or what Elio remembered of it. "She talked about you twice. The first time I was ten. I found a photograph in her drawer. A boy on a Vespa. She said, 'That's someone I knew before your father.' She put the photograph back and closed the drawer. I knew better than to ask again. The second time was in February. She was in bed. She said, 'Clara, I need you to do something for me. There's a box in the wardrobe. The letters are for Elio Moretti. He lives in San Prospero. Find him. Send them. Then wait.'"

"Wait for what?"

"For you to open them." Clara took a sip of coffee. "She said, 'He will be stubborn. He will not open them right away. Wait. Then go and see him.'"

Elio looked at the window. The geraniums were red against the glass. "She was right."

"She usually was." Clara set down her cup. "She died in March. At home. I was with her."

"I'm sorry."

"Thank you." Clara's voice was steady. "She was eighty-two. She had a good life. She taught primary school for forty years. She married my father, Guido, in 1968. He was an engineer. He was kind, and quiet, and he made her laugh. He died ten years ago. She had me, and a balcony full of plants, and a group of friends she played cards with on Thursdays. She was happy. Not every day. But she was happy."

Elio nodded. "Good," he said. He meant it.

Clara asked to see the garden. They went out through the kitchen door and stood among the tomatoes. The sun was warm. The geraniums were red against the wall. Clara touched a leaf and rubbed her fingers together.

"My mother had geraniums," she said. "On the balcony. She said they were the only plants she could not kill. She talked to them. I thought she was crazy. Then I started doing it too."

"What did she say to them?"

"Mostly complaints. About the weather. About the government. About me." Clara smiled. "She said the red ones were the most stubborn. Like her."

Elio looked at the geraniums. "Rina planted these," he said. "The first spring we lived here. She said everything dies, but that's not a reason not to plant it."

Clara nodded. She did not say anything. They stood in the garden for a while without speaking. A bee went from flower to flower. The fig tree cast a small, hard shadow. Then Clara said, "Show me the rest of the house."

They went back inside. Clara looked at the photographs on the sideboard, the blue cardigan on the hook, the clock that ran fast.

"She never stopped talking about San Prospero," Clara said. "Not you. The town. The fountain. The bakery. She said the bread here tasted like the bread her grandmother made. When I was a child, we came here once. We stayed at a hotel near the station. She never brought me to this street."

"She came here?"

"Twice. The last time was six years ago. We were on our way to the station. She wanted to see the piazza. We drove through the roundabout. There was a man in a green Panda. She said, 'That's him.' I said, 'Who?' She said, 'Nobody. An old friend.' She didn't wave. I asked her why. She said it was too late."

Elio remembered a day six years ago. He had been circling the roundabout, though he could not remember why. Perhaps he had been to the cemetery. Perhaps he had been to the hardware store. He remembered a car with a woman driving and an old woman in the passenger seat. He had not looked at them. He had taken the fourth exit.

He remembered the car because it had a dent in the passenger door and a rosary hanging from the mirror. He remembered the old woman's hand on the window, pale and thin. He had thought: someone is going to the station. He had not thought: Ada. He had not thought anything. He had been late for lunch.

"She saw me," he said.

"She saw you."

They sat in silence. The clock ticked fast. Clara looked at the photograph on the sideboard. "Your wife?"

"Rina."

"She's beautiful."

"She was."

"My mother would have liked her."

Elio looked at the photograph. Rina was laughing, her head thrown back, her hands in the pockets of her apron. It was taken at the sea, the year they retired. "I think they would have understood each other," he said.

Clara stayed for an hour. She told him about Ada's last winter: the garden she could not keep up with, the books she read again, the way she asked for apricot jam on everything, even pasta. She told him that Ada had been afraid of dying but not of death.

"She was afraid of the pain," Clara said. "And of leaving me. She was not afraid of what came after. She said she had been loved by two good men and had raised one good daughter, and if there was a God, she had a few questions for him, but she was willing to wait."

Ada had spent her last weeks in the chair by the window, with the lemon tree in a pot and a stack of exercise books from her old classroom. She corrected the grammar in the newspaper. She watched the pigeons on the roof. She made Clara drive her to the sea one last time, in October, and they sat in the car with the heater on and looked at the water.

"It's still there," Ada said.

"What did you expect?" Clara said.

"I expected it to be smaller," Ada said. "Everything else is."

Clara told him that Ada had made her promise not to read the letter. "I kept my promise," Clara said. "It's still sealed. I don't know what she wrote. I only know she wanted you to have it."

When she left, she stood on the doorstep and looked at him. "The box is in the trunk, isn't it?"

Elio nodded.

Clara laughed. It was a small laugh, but it was Ada's. "You really are stubborn."

"I know."

"I'm staying one more night. At the bed-and-breakfast by the station. If you want to talk. After." She did not say after what. She walked to her car, a small gray Fiat, and drove away.

Elio stood in the doorway until the car disappeared around the corner. Then he went into the kitchen and looked at the clock. It was four in the afternoon. The house was quiet. The box was in the car. The car was in the driveway. The driveway was ten meters from the door.

He thought about calling Marco. He thought about calling Giulia. He thought about the things he would say: a woman came today, her name is Clara, she is Ada's daughter, Ada is dead, Ada wrote me a letter, I have not read it. None of it made sense in the order he would have to say it. He put the phone back in his pocket.

He sat at the table and waited for the light to change.

## VI. The Package

At eleven o'clock that night, Elio went out to the garage.

The Panda was dark green in the moonlight, the color of a river at night. He opened the trunk. The parcel was where he had left it, under the blanket and the tin of motor oil. It was smaller than he remembered. He carried it into the kitchen and put it on the table.

He cut the string with a pair of scissors. The paper opened like a flower. Inside was a wooden box, the kind that once held cigars, with a brass clasp. He opened the clasp.

The box smelled of cedar and old paper. The clasp was loose. Inside, everything was wrapped in tissue paper that had yellowed at the folds. Clara had packed it carefully: the letters first, then the photograph, then the small things, each in its own nest. Elio thought of her doing it at a kitchen table in Bologna, with the box open and her mother's voice in her head. He had to stop for a moment and put his hand on the table.

On top was a note in Clara's round handwriting.

*Signor Moretti, these are the things my mother asked me to send you. She wrote the letter last September. She made me promise not to read it. I have kept my promise. I hope you will read it. — Clara*

Under the note were his letters.

They were tied with a red ribbon, the kind that comes on a box of pastries. Some of the envelopes were sealed. Some were open, the paper worn soft at the folds. Elio untied the ribbon and spread them on the table. He recognized his own handwriting: the blue ink, the careful loops, the crossings-out. There were letters he had forgotten writing. There were letters he had written in anger. There was the last one, the one that said, *If you want me to stop, tell me.* It was still sealed. She had never opened it.

He remembered writing that one. He had come home from the garage at midnight and sat in the kitchen with the light off, and written it by the streetlamp through the window. He had wanted to sound calm. He had wanted to sound like a man who could survive an answer. The letter had taken him three hours. It had taken her a lifetime not to open it.

Under the letters was a photograph. Ada at eighteen, sitting on the rim of the fountain, laughing at something outside the frame. She wore the blue dress. The bottle of aranciata was beside her. On the back, in her handwriting: *For Elio, summer 1962.*

He remembered the day she had given him the photograph. It was the week before she left. They were sitting on the fountain rim, and a man with a camera was taking pictures of tourists. Ada had asked him to take one of her, then one of both of them. Elio had never seen the second photograph. He wondered if it was in the box, and then he did not want to look. One was enough. One was already too much.

There was the brass washer on its red string. It had tarnished. There was a pressed cornflower, faded to the color of old paper. There was a small blue airmail envelope, sealed, with his name on it in Ada's handwriting. The ink was slightly shaky.

The washer was the one he had polished on the buffing wheel, the one she had worn around her neck until the string turned gray. He could still feel the weight of it in his palm, warm from her skin. The cornflower was the one he had tucked into her coat pocket at the station. She had pressed it between the pages of a book and kept it for sixty years. He did not know what book. He hoped it was *Il Gattopardo*. He hoped the prince had been kind to it.

Elio sat down. He looked at the envelope for a long time. Then he opened it.

The letter was six pages, written on both sides in a fine, slanting hand.

> Dear Elio,
>
> I have been thinking about water. The fountain in Piazza Grande, the way it sounded at night when the piazza was empty. The river where we took off our shoes. The rain on the balcony in Bologna, which is a different sound entirely. I have been thinking about the way water keeps moving, even when it seems to be still.
>
> I am dying, Elio. Not today, I hope. But soon enough that I have started to say the things I should have said years ago. The doctor is kind and vague. Clara is brave and pretending. I have made a list of things to do. Most of them are small: pay the electricity, give away my clothes, write this letter. The list makes me feel useful. It does not make me feel less afraid.
>
> First, the letters. I know you wrote to me. I did not know for a long time. My father intercepted them. He had the postman bring them to his office. He told me you had stopped writing. He told me you had found someone else. I believed him because I was eighteen and because he was my father and because I could not imagine that you would choose me over the life he wanted for me. When he died, I found them in his desk. Thirty-one letters, Elio. Some of them still sealed. I read the ones he had opened, one after another, until the light came. The sealed ones I left sealed. I told myself I would open them later, when I was braver. I was forty-two years old, a wife, a mother, and I cried like a girl.
>
> I wrote the letter that said please don't write to me anymore because my father told me you had asked him to tell me to stop. I did not know it was a lie. I thought I was giving you what you wanted. I have been angry at him for many years. I have also forgiven him. He was a man of his time, and he thought he was protecting me, and he was wrong. I hope you can forgive him too. I hope you can forgive me for believing him.
>
> I thought about writing to you after I found the letters. I wrote a hundred letters in my head. I never sent one. I was married, and you were married, and I did not know if you would want to hear from me. I told myself that loving you from a distance was enough. It was not enough, but it was what I had.
>
> I want you to know about my life. I married Guido in 1968. He was an engineer with kind hands and a quiet laugh. He loved me. I loved him. We had Clara. I taught school for forty years. I had a balcony with geraniums and a lemon tree in a pot. I went to the sea every August. I was happy, Elio. Not every day. But I was happy. I did not spend my life waiting for you. I lived it. But there was a room in me that stayed yours. I don't think that is a betrayal. I think it is just how a heart is built.
>
> Guido knew about you. Not everything. He knew there was someone before him, and he knew I did not want to talk about it. He never pressed. He was a man who believed that a person's past was their own business, which is a rare thing in a husband. He made me laugh. He made me coffee every morning and burned the toast every Sunday. When Clara was born, he held her in the hospital corridor and cried, and then pretended he had something in his eye. He was not you. I did not want him to be. I wanted him to be Guido, and he was, fully and stubbornly, until the end. I miss him. I miss him in a different room of the same heart.
>
> Clara is the best of me. She has my eyes and her father's patience. She teaches mathematics at a school in Bologna, which I pretend to disapprove of because I wanted her to teach literature, but she is better at numbers than I ever was at anything. She has a partner named Francesca, whom I like very much, and a cat named Dante, whom I do not. She does not know about you. I am telling you now, in this letter, because I am too much of a coward to tell her. She will read it after I am gone, or she will not read it at all. I have asked her not to. I hope she keeps her promise. I hope she breaks it. Both things are true.
>
> The children I taught are grown now. Some of them are grandparents. One of them writes to me at Christmas. He is a dentist in Modena and he still cannot spell. I think of the classroom every day. The smell of chalk, the window with the geraniums, the small hands raised in the air. I was not a great teacher. I was a patient one. I think patience is worth more. I hope so. It is what I have.
>
> I am afraid, Elio. I am afraid of the pain and of the dark and of missing Clara's life. I am afraid that I did not live enough, or that I lived too carefully, or that the careful life was the only one I could have survived. I am afraid that you will read this and be sad. I am afraid that you will not read it at all. I am afraid that I waited too long. I am afraid that waiting was the whole point.
>
> But I am not afraid of the fountain. I am not afraid of the river. I am not afraid of the boy who fixed the pump and looked at his shoes. I have carried him with me for sixty years, and he has been good company. I hope you have been happy, Elio. I hope you have been loved. I hope you have planted geraniums and complained about the government and eaten peaches by the sea. I hope you have taken the long way home.
>
> Six years ago I saw you at the roundabout. Clara was driving me to the station. You were in a green Panda. I knew your hands on the wheel before I knew your face. You took the fourth exit. You always took the long way. I wanted to ask Clara to stop. I did not. I told myself it was too late, and that I had no right to disturb your life, and that you might not remember me. All of those things were true, and none of them was the reason. The reason was that I was afraid.
>
> I am sending you the letters because I want you to have the whole story. Not to make you sad. I want you to know that you were loved, fully, by someone who was not brave enough to come back. I want you to know that it was real. I want you to know that the boy on the Vespa was not a mistake, or a summer, or a thing to be put in a shoebox and forgotten. He was the first person who ever asked me what I wanted and then listened to the answer.
>
> Do you remember the roundabout? The first time we went around it on your father's Vespa, you pointed at the exits and said, one goes to the cemetery, one to the station, one home, one to nowhere. I asked which one we were taking. You said, the long way. You always said that.
>
> Take the third exit, Elio. Go home. I did.
>
> With all my love,
>
> Ada

Elio read the letter twice. The first time he read it fast, the way a man runs through a house to see if anyone is inside. The second time he read it slowly, the way a man walks through the same house after everyone has gone.

Then he put the letter down and cried. He cried the way he had not cried since he was nineteen, under a car in his father's garage. He cried for the boy he had been and the girl she had been. He cried for the letters in the desk. He cried for Rina, who had known there was a shoebox and had loved him anyway. He cried for Clara, who had kept her promise. He cried for the life he had lived and the life he had not, and for the fact that both of them had been real.

When he was finished, he blew his nose and looked at the kitchen clock. It was almost two in the morning. The clock ticked fast. He laughed, once, at nothing.

He looked at Rina's photograph on the sideboard. "She wrote to me," he said. "After all this time. She wrote to me."

Rina looked back at him from the sea, laughing.

He took the letter and read a paragraph aloud to her. Then another. He read the part about the room in Ada's heart, and the part about the fountain, and the part about the third exit. His voice broke on the last line. He read it again, steadier. Rina listened. She was a good listener. She had always been a good listener, even when she disagreed with him, even when she was angry, even when she was dying. He had never told her that. He told her now.

"I should have told you," he said. "About the box. About all of it."

The photograph did not answer. It did not need to.

He took Ada's photograph and put it on the sideboard next to Rina's. The two women looked out at the kitchen: one young and laughing on a fountain, one older and laughing at the sea. Elio stood back and looked at them. He did not feel torn in half. He felt, for the first time in weeks, like a man who knew where he lived.

He sat in the armchair with the letter on his knees. He did not mean to sleep. He slept anyway. In the morning, the letter was still there, and the light was gray, and the clock was ticking fast. He had slept in the armchair. His neck was stiff. The house smelled of cold coffee and old paper. He stood up, and for the first time in three weeks, he knew what he was going to do.

## VII. The Last Lap

He shaved. He put on a clean shirt, the pale blue one Rina had bought him for his eightieth birthday. He made coffee and drank it standing at the window, looking at the garden. The tomatoes were wet with dew. The fig tree was full of small hard fruit. The geraniums were red and shameless.

The town was waking up. A dog barked somewhere on the Via Emilia. A truck went past with a load of crates. The baker's shutters rolled up with a clatter. Elio listened to the sounds the way a man listens to a language he has not spoken in years. He had heard them every morning of his life. This morning they sounded like a welcome.

He put the wooden box on the sideboard, next to the photographs. He put the brass washer in a small white saucer, where he could see it. He did not put the box back in the trunk. He left Ada's letter open on the kitchen table.

At nine o'clock he drove to the roundabout.

He took the long way to get there, past the cemetery and the station, though the roundabout was five minutes from his house. He wanted to see the town first. The station was empty. The clock above the ticket window still had a cracked face. The cemetery gate was open, and someone had cut the grass. The bakery on Via Garibaldi had its shutters up, and the smell of bread was already in the street. He drove slowly. He was not postponing anything now. He was saying good morning.

The morning was clear. The fields were green. The poplars were full of light. The roundabout was empty except for the pigeon on the bayonet. Elio went around once. The first exit: the cemetery, where Rina was not. The second exit: the station, where Ada had left on a train. The third exit: home. The fourth exit: nowhere.

He went around a second time. He thought about Ada's letter. He thought about the room in her heart that had stayed his, and the room in his heart that had stayed hers, and the life he had built around that room with Rina. He thought about Clara, waiting at the bed-and-breakfast. He thought about the third exit, patient as a closed door.

On the third lap, he took it.

He did not hesitate. The indicator clicked. The Panda leaned into the turn, and the road home opened between the poplars, and the white house with the green shutters came into view. He felt nothing dramatic. He felt like a man who had remembered where he was going.

The road home was narrow and lined with poplars. The light came through the leaves in pieces. He drove slowly. He passed the bakery, where Ada's aunt Iole had lived above the shop. He passed the garage that had been his father's, now a phone shop with a plastic sign. He passed the piazza, where the fountain was still running. He did not stop. He would come back later, on foot, and sit on the rim.

At home, he parked the Panda in the driveway and left the trunk empty. He watered the geraniums. Then he came into the kitchen and made another pot of coffee. He stopped in the doorway and looked at the room. The table, the two chairs, the clock, the blue cardigan on the hook. "I'm home, Rina," he said. It was not a sad thing to say. It was a fact, like the weather. Then he called Clara.

"Signor Moretti?"

"Clara. It's Elio."

There was a pause. "You opened it."

"I opened it."

Another pause. "Are you all right?"

"I am," he said. "I am. Would you like to come for breakfast?"

She arrived half an hour later with a loaf of bread from the bakery and another jar of apricot jam. They sat at the kitchen table. The morning light came through the window and made a bright square on the oilcloth. Clara looked at the two photographs on the sideboard. She did not say anything. She smiled.

They ate bread and jam and drank coffee. Clara told him about Ada's last weeks: the card game she insisted on playing even when she could not hold the cards, the way she corrected the nurse's grammar, the afternoon she made Clara read her the newspaper and then argued with every word of it. Elio laughed. Clara laughed. The clock ticked fast.

Clara asked about Rina. Elio told her about the geraniums and the dog and the way Rina had danced at their wedding. He told her about Marco and Giulia, and the grandchildren, and the Sunday phone calls. Clara told him about Francesca, and the cat, and the flat in Bologna with the balcony.

"It has geraniums," she said. "My mother's. I took them when she died. They are still alive. I talk to them. I complain about the government."

"She would be proud," Elio said.

"She would be surprised," Clara said. "I have never managed to keep anything alive before."

They sat for a while. The sun moved across the oilcloth. The clock ticked fast.

"What will you do now?" Clara asked.

Elio thought about it. The garden needed tying up. The car needed washing. There was a letter from the comune about the road tax. There was a book on the sideboard that he had been meaning to read.

"I think I'll take the third exit," he said.

Clara looked at him. Then she laughed, and it was Ada's laugh, low and sudden, like a drawer opening. "Every day?"

"Every day."

She raised her coffee cup. "To the third exit."

He raised his. "To the third exit."

Clara reached into her handbag and took out a pen and a small notebook. She wrote something and tore out the page. "This is my address," she said. "And my telephone number. In Bologna. If you ever want to come. There is a spare room. It looks onto the courtyard with the lemon tree. It was my mother's room."

Elio looked at the paper. The handwriting was round and careful, the letters standing upright, the ink blue. It was the handwriting from the parcel. He folded the paper and put it in his shirt pocket.

"I don't travel much," he said.

"You used to," she said.

He laughed. "I used to take the train."

"There is still a train."

He nodded. "I'll think about it."

"Good," she said. "Thinking is a start."

After she left, Elio stood in the doorway and watched her car go around the corner. Then he went back into the kitchen. He did not put the letter away yet. He walked into town. The morning was warm. The piazza was almost empty. A man was sweeping the steps of the church. The fountain was running, the water clear and loud in the stone basin. Elio sat on the rim where Ada had sat with her book and her bottle of aranciata. He put his hand in the water. It was cold.

He thought about the boy who had fixed the pump. He thought about the girl who had said, 'I like you, Elio Moretti.' He thought about the life that had happened instead: Rina, the children, the garage, the geraniums, the sea. He did not weigh one against the other. He had stopped doing that. They were both his.

Teresa Bissi came across the piazza with her shopping bag. She stopped when she saw him. "Elio," she said. "You're sitting."

"I'm sitting," he said.

"Are you all right?"

"I am," he said. "I took the third exit."

She looked at him for a moment. Then she nodded, as if this made perfect sense. "About time," she said. She went on across the piazza, and Elio sat by the fountain until the church clock struck eleven.

When he came back, he put the letter in the wooden box with the letters and the photograph and the brass washer and the pressed cornflower. He tied the box with the red ribbon. He put it on the top shelf of the wardrobe, next to the shoebox, where he could reach it if he wanted to.

Then he went out to the garden and tied up the tomatoes.

The third exit was the one that went home. That morning, Elio Moretti took it.
