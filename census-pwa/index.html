a game about sampling

CENSUS

You are the chief enumerator. Nobody will tell you how many people live in the city until after you have committed a number to paper. Everything on screen is real sampling arithmetic.

Tap a ward to select it, then send enumerators. Each visit costs a day's pay and one day of your deadline.

How it works

You never see the population. You see a city divided into wards, a purse, and a deadline. Each time you send enumerators into a ward they knock on doors, some are answered, and they come back with a number of people per house.

Your return estimates each visited ward by scaling that average up to the ward's houses. Wards you never visited are omitted from your total entirely — the page says which ones, because pretending a hole is not there would be the one dishonest thing this game refuses to do.

Three things will cost you the return

  • Not visiting. A ward you skipped is not a ward with zero people. It is a gap in your figure, and it grows the gap.
  • Who refuses. If the households that do not answer the door are systematically larger than the ones that do, your average household is too small and your city comes out too small. More enumerators will not fix that; only noticing it will.
  • Over-claiming. You may state 99% confidence instead of 80%, and the Board will accept the more impressive number. The interval gets wider, and a wider interval can rescue an estimate that would otherwise be rejected.

The maths is not decorative

The margin of error is z·√(p(1−p)/n) over the households that answered, added in quadrature to the sampling error in the average household size. The result is a 95% interval that contains the truth about 92% of the time when nothing is biasing your sample — and about 75% of the time when non-response is selective. That gap is the point of the whole game.
